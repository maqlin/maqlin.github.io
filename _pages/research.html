I study how galaxies and dark matter haloes assemble inside the cosmic web. The work falls into two directions.

  1. Galaxy formation and evolution
  2. Dark matter haloes within large-scale structure

Galaxy formation and evolution

How halo conditions, together with baryonic physics, set the structure of galaxies, from dwarf galaxies to the most massive systems across cosmic time.

Interpreting the strong clustering of ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) by halo spin bias

arXiv ADS Publisher (accepted by MNRAS)

Isolated blue UDGs in SDSS cluster as strongly as massive groups, a signal that ΛCDM models do not reproduce. Using the TNG300-1-Dark simulation, we find that spin bias depends on how halo spin is defined. We then build a mock catalog that reproduces the strong clustering of UDGs with spin bias under a new definition. Our results suggest that the environment can shape the sizes of dwarf galaxies.

Figure: New spin bias can well interpret strong clustering of UDG.

Two-phase formation of Milky Way-like galaxies

arXiv ADS Publisher

In the FIRE-2 zoom-in simulations, Milky Way-mass galaxies switch from a hot, bulge-dominated, bursty phase to a cold, disk-dominated, steady phase. The switch lines up with the host halo's transition from fast to slow accretion. Once fast accretion stalls and feedback lowers the gas supply, a stellar disk can grow.

Figure: quadrant diagram (accretion rate vs gas fraction), color coded by hotness of galaxies.

Three-stage assembly of brightest central galaxies (BCG)

Ma et al. (in prep.)

With the COLIBRE simulation, we find that the assembly of BCGs exhibits three distinct stages: a stage at \(z\gtrsim 6\) where star formation is extended within the host halos, a stage at \(2\lesssim z\lesssim 6\) where star formation becomes concentrated and a thick disk forms (\(M_\star \approx 10^{10.5}\,M_\odot\)), and a stage at \(z\lesssim 2\) where accretion of ex-situ stars extends and heats up the galaxies.

Figure: Evolution of an example BCG.

Dark matter haloes within large-scale structure

How the anisotropic cosmic web affects the evolution of dark matter haloes — how they grow, how they are oriented, and how their satellites are distributed.

Lopsided satellites around paired haloes

Observation part

arXiv ADS Publisher

In SDSS, satellites around galaxy-group pairs are lopsided: they prefer the region between the two central galaxies, and they bulge along the line that joins them. Both trends strengthen as the pair separation and halo mass increase. Halo-based mock catalogs reproduce the measurements. Overlapping two mass-matched haloes, once their orientations are aligned with the pair axis, accounts for the signal, so it follows from large-scale structure in ΛCDM.

Figure: schematic of a halo pair and the satellite position angle.

Simulation part

arXiv ADS Publisher

In TNG300-1-Dark, the three-dimensional distribution of subhaloes around halo pairs combines a lopsided excess between the two haloes with a bulging excess along the pair axis. Lopsidedness comes mainly from halo overlap and grows with primary mass. Bulging traces alignment with cosmic filaments and is stronger at larger separation. Projecting from 3D to 2D strongly suppresses the bulging signal.

Anisotropic secondary bias of dark matter haloes

arXiv ADS Publisher (submitted to ApJ)

Using TNG300-1-Dark at z = 0, we measure anisotropic secondary bias: how halo clustering depends on properties other than mass, and how that dependence changes with direction relative to the halo major axis. The anisotropic signal itself is strong for spin and shape: slowly rotating and more elongated haloes align more strongly with filaments. Such effect can't be explained by tidal anisotropy, indicating the complex interplay between halo growth and environment.

Figure: Correlation between secondary properties and alignment as a function of halo mass.

A fly-by origin of secondary bias

We construct a chain for how an anisotropic environment modulates halo growth and affects their secondary properties, leading to their secondary bias across the whole mass range and the whole set of secondary properties.
